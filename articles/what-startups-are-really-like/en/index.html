Translation and notes

A readable adaptation of the article's core ideas, with the original source clearly credited.

This essay is useful because it removes some of the glamour from startup work without making the work smaller. A company is not only an idea, a product, or a financing path. After the start, founders discover that cofounder trust, emotional endurance, small decisions, user contact, and luck all become operating variables.

The first operating lesson is to treat the cofounder relationship as core infrastructure. Skill matters, but pressure tests character and commitment faster than a resume can. A relationship that works in ordinary projects may behave differently when money is short, users are confused, investors are slow, and the team is tired.

The second lesson is that startups compress life and time. Progress, fear, excitement, and disappointment can rotate within the same day. A serious team has to separate mood from evidence, avoid making major decisions only at emotional highs or lows, and manage founder energy as a limited resource.

The third lesson is that most progress is incremental. Launch something minimal, start the user conversation, keep changing the hypothesis when reality demands it, and do not wait for one feature, one deal, or one investor to rescue the company. Persistence matters, but only when it is paired with flexibility and a willingness to keep learning.

Selected advice from the article

  1. Test a possible cofounder through real stressful work before formal commitment.
  2. Create a decision rhythm that keeps emotional swings from driving the company.
  3. Ship the smallest complete version that can start a real user conversation.
  4. Measure customer acquisition separately from product quality.
  5. Plan cash and deals with conservative assumptions about what you do not control.

Operating takeaways

  • Cofounder character is an operating risk, not a soft issue.
  • Founder energy and emotional volatility need active management.
  • Small continuous improvements usually matter more than a single breakthrough.
  • User acquisition is a separate discipline, not an automatic reward for good product work.
  • Community can improve judgment by normalizing hard problems and exposing better choices.
