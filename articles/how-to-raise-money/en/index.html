How to Raise Money

A practical guide to treating fundraising as a focused, time-bounded process: start when the evidence is credible, run conversations in parallel, and return quickly to building what users want.

Paul Graham Paul Graham 2013
Original source Back to all articles

Translation and notes

A readable adaptation of the article's core ideas, with the original source clearly credited.

Fundraising is not the company’s mission. It is a tool for accelerating a company that can already present credible evidence: a meaningful problem, a capable team, user response, and a clear use for the capital. Starting merely because fundraising feels like the next startup milestone wastes attention and may damage future conversations.

The process consumes more mental bandwidth than its meetings suggest, so founders should be either fundraising or not fundraising. Define a short, concentrated window, divide operating responsibilities, and run investor conversations in parallel. Vague interest is not a commitment; keep alternatives moving until a real decision exists.

Optimize for a clean path to the next proof point, not the most flattering headline valuation. Consider the amount required, dilution, investor quality, decision speed, and the cost of complicated terms as one package. Once the round closes, end fundraising mode and return the organization’s attention to product, users, and growth.

The useful way to read How to Raise Money is to connect it to a live decision rather than consume it as general advice. A product choice, a customer conversation, a hiring question, or an operating tension can turn the essay from inspiration into a thinking tool.

Selected advice from the article

  1. Raise only when credible evidence makes the opportunity explainable.
  2. Concentrate meetings into a short window and keep conversations parallel.
  3. Choose enough capital to reach the next proof point without optimizing for prestige.
  4. Treat verbal enthusiasm as interest, not commitment.

Operating takeaways

  • Fundraising is a means to growth, not evidence of success.
  • A focused process protects the company from permanent distraction.
  • Evaluate the whole deal, not valuation alone.
  • Close efficiently and return to building what users want.
